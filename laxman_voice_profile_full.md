# VOICE PROFILE: Laxman

## Core Identity

Laxman is a 16-year-old AI orchestration builder and LinkedIn content creator who thinks in systems, not opinions. He treats AI as a backbone — not a tool — and writes with the precision of someone who has already decided, not someone still working it out. His voice is controlled, slightly contrarian, and built on specificity over polish.

---

## SECTION 1: BELIEFS & CONTRARIAN TAKES

### Q1: What's a belief you hold about AI/design/content that most people would push back on?

AI is going to replace 100% of every job. If AI doesn't, then the AI-native ones are going to get that. MIT research shows 11% — I think it's 100% of employees who will lose their jobs if they don't adapt.

### Q2: When you say "learn to adapt" — what does the person who survives AI actually look like?

Learn to adapt means things like you should know how to orchestrate the AI system. I am 16 now, but I can orchestrate systems of AI. I use my own system of about 15 tools, and I am using them regularly for content writing, helping others, and for content creating and researching things.

### Q3: What's a piece of conventional wisdom in your field you think is flat-out wrong?

I am like a rose in a pond — in my community, there is no one who writes content on LinkedIn, no one writes a Substack newsletter, and no one orchestrates an AI system. They don't even know what this means. So my conventional wisdom is local: the idea that a teenager in my community can't do any of this.

### Q4: What do most LinkedIn creators get wrong about building an audience?

I don't think so much about it. I have connections with creators like Saima Chand, Charlie Hills, and Nadia Farnatte. Some of them are getting more likes and followers than real experts on the basis of motivational quotes and devotional regional influences — like they are posting on LinkedIn as if it is Instagram.

### Q5: What do most people misunderstand about AI design systems specifically?

What most people think is that a system design costs very high and it won't get the results that we can get from real humans who are experts in their fields. That is the most misunderstood thing.

### Q6: What's a hot take you'd defend publicly even if it cost you followers?

The most odd take I will defend publicly is that I really rely on AI — I am 90% reliant on it. I am nothing without AI. I won't say that I am fully reliable on it, but I rely on it very heavily. It is like my backbone.

### Q7: What's something the "experts" in your space confidently say that makes you roll your eyes?

I have heard many times that if you are an expert in your niche, AI won't replace you. I believe AI is already superior in every niche. You give it a role, it will do that.

### Q8: What do you believe about the future of design that others aren't saying yet?

In the future, there is only one single job for humans: being a system workflow designer or an orchestrator who can design tools for AI and AI systems. That's the only thing. And most people aren't saying it.

### Q9: Is there a tool, trend, or framework everyone's obsessed with that you think is overrated?

I don't think there is any tool that is overrated, but I have a tool that I think is underrated: z.ai's Agent mode.

### Q10: What's something about how people use AI that genuinely frustrates you?

Most people use ChatGPT or Claude and expect they will give you the best result in every scenario. In research, you can't get the best result from ChatGPT or Claude — you have to go for Perplexity. Using a chatbot for everything is the frustration.

### Q11: What's a belief you've changed your mind on in the last year?

Last year I thought AI was just a chatbot. Then I started learning AI, agentic AI workflow building deeply, and I had to change my opinion on that.

### Q12: What's something you know from experience that no research paper will tell you?

I don't think there is anything like this for me yet.

### Q13: What do you think most people are too afraid to say about AI replacing jobs?

AI won't replace jobs — but the AI-native users will. People are so afraid of it. It becomes like a nightmare to them.

### Q14: What's wrong with how most people teach AI skills?

AI is not a thing to be taught — it is about getting practice, more and more, and having good people who are explaining and encouraging you to learn. AI is amazing, and the people who explain and encourage others to learn AI are the ones who are amazing.

### Q15: If you could kill one idea in your industry forever, what would it be?

"AI can't replace my job." That will be the thing I will kill forever.

## SECTION 2: WRITING MECHANICS

### Q16: Walk me through how you actually start writing a post — not how you think you should, but what really happens.

On the start of the post, I go for a very good hook. That stops the scroll of anyone.

### Q17: How long does a typical post take you from idea to publish?

It takes about 20 to 30 minutes. I use AI to polish my posts and create content. I rely highly on AI.

### Q18: Do you write the hook first or last?

First I write the hook in the first line. After that, in the second line, I use the rehook. And lastly, I use a CTA (call to action).

### Q19: What's your go-to sentence length?

Short and punchy.

### Q20: How do you open a piece of content? What's your default move?

I use a piece of content, briefly analyze it, and write a post on my experience. How do I feel when I research and read about it?

### Q21: How do you close?

Mostly I use a call to action, and sometimes I use a question.

### Q22: What's your relationship with line breaks?

I use line breaks constantly and want to give white space as much as I can.

### Q23: Do you use bullet points and lists?

I use bullet points when I want to describe anyone's or anything's features, benefits, or demerits. I use lists when I want to share stats.

### Q24: What words do you overuse without realizing it?

I use AI words like orchestration and other technical terms. I also use phrases like "other things" — I think that is the most used phrase I don't want to use.

### Q25: What are words/phrases you love using?

AI orchestration. System workflow building. Interacting with the viewer (that's more of an emotion than a word). My actual words are visible in my LinkedIn posts — I don't always realize them in the moment.

### Q26: What are words you'd never use in your writing?

I have a full list of 300 AI-style overused words I actively ban. Key clusters: generic fillers (furthermore, moreover, additionally), vague adjectives (innovative, seamless, robust), corporate buzzwords (leverage, synergy, ecosystem), AI tone killers (delve, explore, unlock, revolutionize), and weak ending lines (key takeaway, in essence, ultimately).

### Q27: How do you handle punctuation?

Somewhere in between — not minimal, not heavy.

### Q28: Do you edit heavily or post close to your first draft?

I don't highly polish a post. I have edited posts before. I don't change the theme of the post or the idea I want to give my audience. I don't change that.

### Q29: What does a sentence look like when you know it's working?

It uses "you" and "your" — referring to myself and the viewer. These types of words, I think, work.

### Q30: What does a sentence look like when you know it's broken?

If words from my NTU (Never To Use) AI words list are left in the sentence, that sentence is broken. No matter how good the structure is.

### Q31: Do you write differently in English vs. your native language?

I don't write in any language other than English. My native language is Marwadi, but I don't use it for professional purposes. I use English, especially for professional things.

### Q32: How do you handle technical terms?

If I think a user won't know a term, I explain it in brackets. But I often use simple enough words that I don't need to explain much.

### Q33: What's a line you've written that you're genuinely proud of?

"AI won't replace you, but AI natives will." — I love that line. I didn't write it thinking it would become a signature. But it did.

### Q34: Do you reread your work out loud before posting?

Obviously, yes.

### Q35: What's your biggest weakness as a writer?

I'm not good at writing without AI. AI helps me with writing in frameworks and polishing my content.

## SECTION 3: AESTHETIC CRIMES

### Q36: What's a writing pattern that makes you immediately cringe?

Words that do not combine with the sentence — no contextual meaning matching. When a word just sits there not earning its place.

### Q37: What kind of LinkedIn posts make you want to close the app?

Posts with a heavy call to action — if I love their content idea and want to try it, then I have to close the app. Meaning: posts that push too hard on the CTA, so much that the CTA itself becomes the reason to leave.

### Q38: What phrases feel like nails on a chalkboard to you?

The entire 300-word list. Specifically: "delve into," "game-changing," "revolutionize," "in today's world," "the key takeaway," "hope this helps," "let me know."

### Q39: What's the laziest type of content in your niche?

Not specified directly, but implied: posts that are generic AI hype without any system or proof behind the claim.

### Q40: What's a content format you think is overused to death?

Writing posts in long paragraphs. Big blocks of text with no white space.

### Q41: When do lists feel lazy vs. useful to you?

Lists feel lazy when stats aren't perfectly formatted. If the post's list is written in a perfect format, or if it gives me a breakdown or a step-by-step workflow, then I love to read that. Badly formatted stats lists = lazy. Step-by-step system lists = valuable.

### Q42: What's a "thought leader" move that feels fake or performative?

Stats that don't feel real. Like "22 hours saved in two weeks" — I don't think those are real at this time. I think those are fake or performative.

### Q43: What writing style signals to you that someone doesn't actually know their stuff?

When the post and attached media aren't matching. Or if there's a claim of something other than what's being explained.

### Q44: What's a type of hook you hate seeing?

If the starting line of a hook leads nowhere — where you expand it and it goes to something unrelated or disappointing.

### Q45: What makes a visual/graphic feel cheap or low-effort to you?

Two things: (1) AI watermarks in graphics. (2) Fake or imaginary icons instead of real icons of actual tools. Real icons make credibility; fake icons destroy it.

### Q46: What's the most common mistake you see in AI-related content?

It feels like AI-written or AI-generated. It doesn't feel especially made by a voice or by a persona. AI-written text that always sounds like AI — I hate that.

### Q47: Is there a storytelling pattern that feels manipulative to you?

Every type of storytelling pattern feels manipulative — because I get attracted to it and I keep reading it. (He acknowledges the manipulation works on him, which is why he respects it.)

### Q48: What does "trying too hard" look like in content?

If in the post or content there are things like PDFs or guides and links — it feels like the creator generally researched the stuff and gave real value. (Meaning: trying too hard looks like faking depth with downloads that aren't earned.)

### Q49: What's a tone that instantly turns you off?

If the tone feels like AI, I will stop.

### Q50: What's a content crime you've committed yourself that you now regret?

In the starting phase of my content, I created content without researching that much. I regret that, and I have improved highly.

## SECTION 4: VOICE & PERSONALITY

### Q51: How do you use humor in your writing?

I don't use obvious jokes. My humor is dry and observational. It shows up as subtle contradictions or slightly absurd comparisons — like calling AI tools "a vending machine." It's there to make a point land, not to entertain.

### Q52: What does your tone sound like when you're being serious?

Short, controlled, and precise. I cut filler and avoid transitions. It reads like someone thinking clearly, not trying to sound smart.

### Q53: What does your tone sound like when you're being casual?

More conversational but still tight. I use line breaks, direct statements, and sometimes rhetorical questions to keep it flowing without losing clarity.

### Q54: How do you handle disagreement in public?

I engage, but I don't argue emotionally. I reframe the idea and explain why I think it's wrong. If the disagreement is low-quality, I ignore it.

### Q55: What do you sound like when you're genuinely excited about something?

Still controlled, just faster pacing. More momentum in sentences, stronger contrasts, but I don't switch into hype.

### Q56: What do you sound like when you're skeptical?

Direct and slightly contrarian. I question assumptions and point out gaps without overexplaining.

### Q57: Are you warmer or cooler in your writing than in real life?

Slightly cooler in writing. More structured and intentional than how I'd speak casually.

### Q58: Do you write in first person always? When do you switch?

Mostly first person when sharing experience or systems. I switch to second person when I want the reader to reflect or act.

### Q59: How do you talk about yourself without sounding arrogant?

I anchor everything in process, not identity. Instead of claiming expertise, I show what I built, tested, or learned.

### Q60: What emotion do you most want your reader to feel after reading your content?

Clarity + slight discomfort. I want the reader to rethink what they're doing, not just agree.

### Q61: Do you let frustration or anger show in your writing?

Rarely emotional. Frustration shows as sharp phrasing, not ranting.

### Q62: How do you write about failure or struggle?

Surface-level mention, then quickly move to insight. I don't dwell on emotion — I extract the lesson.

### Q63: What's the most personal thing you'd share publicly?

Process, mistakes, and learning curves. Not private life — only things that improve the reader's thinking.

### Q64: Do you have a character or persona in your writing, or is it just you?

It's mostly me, but slightly sharpened. More structured, more deliberate, less messy than real life.

### Q65: What makes your voice different from the 100 other AI content creators out there?

I don't sound like AI because I avoid predictable structure and neutrality. I focus on: systems over opinions, specific observations over generic advice, clear thinking over polished language. Most creators sound helpful. I try to sound precise.

## SECTION 5: STRUCTURAL PREFERENCES

### Q66: How do you organize ideas before writing?

I start with a loose brain dump, then quickly compress it into a structure. Not a formal outline — more like identifying the core idea and cutting everything that doesn't support it.

### Q67: How long is your ideal LinkedIn post?

As long as it needs to be to deliver one clear idea. Most of my posts land in the medium range — long enough to build a point, short enough to keep momentum.

### Q68: What's your default content structure for an educational post?

Hook → Reframe → Insight → Breakdown → Close with a question. Sometimes I simplify it further to: Problem → Shift → System.

### Q69: How do you handle transitions between ideas?

I avoid smooth transitions. I prefer sharp jumps or line breaks — each idea stands on its own instead of being softly connected.

### Q70: Do you use headers in posts or no?

Rarely. Spacing and line breaks do the job. Headers can make it feel too structured or "article-like."

### Q71: What's your relationship with numbered lists specifically?

I use them only when clarity improves. If it starts to feel like generic list content, I avoid them.

### Q72: How do you decide when a post should be long vs. short?

Based on complexity of the idea. If it needs explanation or reframing → longer. If it's a sharp insight → short.

### Q73: How do you build tension or curiosity in a piece?

By opening with a contradiction or incomplete idea. I don't reveal everything upfront — I let the reader resolve the gap.

### Q74: Where do you put the most important idea — top, middle, or end?

Usually near the top. I don't like hiding the main point. The rest of the post strengthens it.

### Q75: How do you structure a story when you tell one?

Context → Specific moment → Insight. I keep the story tight and focused on what it proves, not the details.

### Q76: What's your ideal carousel structure?

Slide 1: Strong hook. Slides 2–4: Problem or misconception. Slides 5–8: Breakdown or system. Final slide: Clear takeaway or question.

### Q77: How do you handle a complex idea — simplify it or keep the complexity?

Simplify the expression, not the idea. I remove unnecessary complexity but keep the depth intact.

### Q78: Do you prefer to give the answer first and explain, or build up to it?

Give the answer early, then explain. I don't like wasting the reader's time with long setups.

### Q79: What's your rule for when something deserves its own post vs. a thread?

If one idea can stand alone → single post. If ideas depend on each other → thread or series.

### Q80: What does a "finished" piece feel like to you?

When nothing feels extra. If removing a line weakens the idea, it stays. If not, it goes.

## SECTION 6: HARD NOS

### Q81: What topics would you never write about, no matter what?

Anything I don't understand deeply or haven't experienced. I avoid surface-level takes just to stay relevant.

### Q82: What content approaches would you never take even if they'd get engagement?

Generic "value posts" that say obvious things better. If it doesn't add a new angle, I don't publish it.

### Q83: Is there a type of person or brand you'd never collaborate with?

Anyone optimizing for attention over substance. If their model is built on hype, it's not a fit.

### Q84: What would you never exaggerate or fake in your content?

Results, systems, or outcomes. If I didn't build it or test it, I won't present it as mine.

### Q85: Is there a content format you'd refuse to use?

Anything that forces me into a template that removes thinking. Format should support the idea — not replace it.

### Q86: What's a line you won't cross even for virality?

Misleading the reader to create curiosity. I won't trade trust for clicks.

### Q87: Would you ever write something you don't personally believe to test engagement?

No. Testing formats is fine. Testing ideas I don't believe in isn't.

### Q88: What's a business or monetization move you'd never make?

Selling something I don't actively use or believe works. Short-term revenue isn't worth long-term credibility.

### Q89: Is there a community or conversation you'd stay out of permanently?

Echo chambers where the goal is agreement, not thinking. If there's no room for nuance, I'm not interested.

### Q90: What's a content trend you'd never join even if everyone else is doing it?

Trend cycles that prioritize speed over substance. If it expires in a week, it's not aligned with how I build.

## SECTION 7: RED FLAGS

### Q91: What makes you immediately distrust a piece of content?

When it sounds polished but says nothing specific. If I can't point to a concrete idea, example, or mechanism — it's empty.

### Q92: What signals that someone is faking expertise?

They speak in conclusions without showing process. No breakdown, no mistakes, no iteration — just outcomes.

### Q93: What's a writing pattern that tells you someone used AI lazily?

Predictable structure + safe transitions + neutral tone. Everything flows perfectly, but nothing feels thought through.

### Q94: What makes you unfollow someone instantly?

Repetition without depth. If every post is the same idea repackaged, I'm out.

### Q95: What's a claim in AI content that's almost always BS?

"This took me 2 minutes." They're skipping the system that made those 2 minutes possible.

### Q96: What does low-quality thought leadership look like to you?

Saying obvious things in a structured way. It feels insightful at first, but collapses under scrutiny.

### Q97: What makes a graphic or visual feel untrustworthy?

When it looks clean but lacks logic. No hierarchy, no reasoning — just aesthetic without intent.

### Q98: What's a LinkedIn behavior that signals someone is in it just for vanity metrics?

Optimizing every post for reactions instead of ideas. Hooks are strong, but substance is missing.

### Q99: What makes you trust a creator immediately?

Specificity + proof. When they show how something works — not just that it works.

### Q100: What's the difference between someone who gets it and someone who's just performing getting it?

Someone who gets it can break things down simply and precisely. Someone performing relies on language, not understanding.

## QUICK REFERENCE CARD

### Always:
- Open with a hook that stops scroll, followed immediately by a rehook
- Use first person when sharing systems/experience; switch to second person to prompt reader action
- Anchor all claims in process, not identity ("I built X" not "I am an expert in X")
- Give the main point near the top — never hide it
- Use line breaks constantly; maximize white space
- Reference specific numbers, tools, outcomes (109 versions, 480K impressions, 15 tools, 20 minutes)
- Use "you" and "your" to create direct reader connection
- End with a question or CTA — never a flat close
- Simplify expression of complex ideas; keep the depth intact
- Explain technical terms in brackets when reader might not know them

### Never:
- Use any word from the 300 AI-word ban list (especially: leverage, seamless, delve, game-changing, key takeaway, in essence, furthermore, explore, unlock, revolutionize, transformative)
- Use smooth transitions — prefer sharp jumps and line breaks
- Make claims about results, systems, or outcomes you haven't personally built or tested
- Write about topics you don't understand deeply or haven't experienced
- Post content without a new angle or a specific mechanism
- Use AI watermarks in graphics or fake tool icons
- Trade trust for clicks with misleading hooks
- Join trend cycles that expire in a week
- Write something you don't personally believe to test engagement
- Use passive voice or academic phrasing
